\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.33\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{detection / diagnosis}: predict discrete disease labels (e.g., fracture, pneumonia, tumor, triage)
\item \textbf{segmentation}: outline a structure or lesion
\item \textbf{measurement / quantification}: measure a number from the image (e.g., bone age, Cobb angle, organ volume, fetal biometry)
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.33\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{outcome prediction}: a future risk, response, or survival estimate from the image (prognosis, radiomics signatures)
\item \textbf{reconstruction / imputation}: generate a better or missing image
\item \textbf{report generation / LLM}: text: draft, summarize, or extract from radiology reports
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.33\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{foundation model / vision-language}: a large pretrained model reused across tasks
\item \textbf{agent / autonomous}: multi-step actions taken without a human in the loop
\item \textbf{workflow / non-interpretive}: protocoling, scheduling, ordering, decision support, education
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\begin{frame}<0>[category=Newsletters]{Story 1: a breakthrough designation is not a clearance}
\relax
{\scriptsize The Imaging Wire, 29 March 2026: ``Ultrasound AI Gets Breakthrough Nod''
(\href{https://theimagingwire.com/newsletter/mammography-use-falls/}{theimagingwire.com})\par}
\medskip
\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What happened}: the FDA granted \textbf{Breakthrough Device Designation} to DeepEcho's Blind Sweep Platform, a prenatal ultrasound AI that talks the operator through a set of blind sweeps and then reads them.
  \item \textbf{What it does}: from B-mode sweeps rather than expert-acquired standard planes, it automates fetal presentation, placental location and gestational age --- the measurements that decide whether a pregnancy needs referral.
  \item \textbf{Why the acquisition end matters}: most fetal AI assumes a sonographer who can already find the view. Moving the AI into acquisition is what makes obstetric ultrasound possible where there is no sonographer, which is where most undetected pathology is.
  \item \textbf{Read the regulatory word carefully}: Breakthrough Designation buys priority FDA interaction, not market authorization. DeepEcho is still working toward clearance, following a separate prenatal algorithm cleared in 2025.
\end{itemize}
\medskip
\textbf{Take-home}: trade-press headlines blur designation, clearance and CE mark. For a children's hospital, only clearance with a pediatric or fetal indication is deployable today.
\end{frame}

(\href{https://www.rsna.org/news/2026/august/ai-challenges-fuel-innovation}{rsna.org})\par}
\medskip
\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What happened}: RSNA looked back at a decade of its Kaggle AI Challenges and argued that the lasting product is not the winning model but the \textbf{curated multinational datasets with expert annotations}, which stay public after the leaderboard closes, plus the open-sourced top solutions.
  \item \textbf{The pediatric one}: the \textbf{2017 Pediatric Bone Age Challenge} drew 700+ entries and remains one of the largest open pediatric imaging datasets (14,236 hand radiographs).
  \item \textbf{The generalization lesson}: Kitamura's team placed third, then revalidated on Brazilian data and found performance did not transfer unchanged --- the same population-shift problem that makes adult-trained tools unsafe in children.
  \item \textbf{The honest caveat}: Pan notes these algorithms target one urgent finding and behave as triage, not as a general assistant --- leaderboard rank does not predict clinical usefulness.
\end{itemize}
\medskip
\textbf{Take-home}: challenges are how pediatric imaging has historically gotten public data. Contributing annotated pediatric cases is a concrete, low-cost way for a children's hospital to move the field.

\begin{frame}[category=Commercial Software]{Product 1: BoneXpert --- the most-validated pediatric AI you cannot buy}
\relax
{\scriptsize Visiana, Denmark (\href{https://bonexpert.com/}{bonexpert.com}) --- bone age / growth\par}
\medskip
\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it does}: automated Greulich--Pyle and Tanner--Whitehouse bone age from a single hand radiograph, plus a \textbf{Bone Health Index} from cortical thickness and an adult-height predictor. Not a second reader --- it replaces the manual rating.
  \item \textbf{Why it is the benchmark}: designed for children from the start, in routine European clinical use since 2009, and the comparator nearly every academic bone-age paper measures itself against. It is the one pediatric AI with a decade of unglamorous production experience behind it.
  \item \textbf{The catch}: CE-marked in Europe; in the United States the vendor labels it \textbf{research use only}. There is no FDA authorization. The only pediatric-specific US bone-age clearance is a different product, EFAI Bonesuite XR Bone Age Pro (ages 2--16, K234042, 2024).
  \item \textbf{What it tells us about the market}: pediatric-native tools exist and work. The bottleneck is a US regulatory and commercial path, not the science.
\end{itemize}
\medskip
\textbf{Take-home}: when someone says ``there is no pediatric AI'', the accurate statement is that the best pediatric AI is not licensed here.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[category=Commercial Software]{Product 2: BoneView --- read the anatomy, not just the age range}
\relax
{\scriptsize Gleamer, France --- fracture / trauma; US clearance K222176 (BoneView 1.1-US, 2023)\par}
\medskip
\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it does}: flags suspected fractures on trauma radiographs as regions of interest beside the image. The international suite also covers dislocations, effusions and focal bone lesions; \textbf{the US clearance is fracture detection only}.
  \item \textbf{The indication is narrower than ``ages 2--21''}: K222176 permits ages 2--21 for \textbf{specified extremity views}, clavicle included. Pelvis, hip, femur, ribs and spine are \textbf{adult-only} in that summary --- a child with a suspected hip or rib fracture is outside the pediatric indication.
  \item \textbf{What the real-life pediatric evidence shows}: the one pediatric ED evaluation of a cleared fracture detector (Ziegner et al., \textit{Eur Radiol} 2025, 1,672 radiographs) found 92\% stand-alone sensitivity but only \textbf{68\% for radial condyle fractures}, and residents' accuracy rose 88\% $\rightarrow$ 90\%.
  \item \textbf{The failure mode to watch}: in 2\% of cases readers abandoned a correct diagnosis after seeing the AI.
\end{itemize}
\medskip
\textbf{Take-home}: the most mature pediatric AI market still needs local validation by anatomy, and a reader policy for disagreement.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[category=Commercial Software]{Product 3: Aidoc BriefCase --- a pediatric label is not pediatric evidence}
\relax
{\scriptsize Aidoc, Israel --- CT/CTA triage and worklist prioritization; e.g.\ K253578 (2026)\par}
\medskip
\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it does}: reads CT and CTA as they arrive and reorders the worklist --- intracranial hemorrhage, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, cervical-spine and rib fractures, free gas. Triage, not diagnosis, is the product.
  \item \textbf{The number that matters here}: Aidoc holds \textbf{23 of the 230 pediatric-label-positive} radiology authorizations in our FDA screen, more than any other company.
  \item \textbf{What that does and does not mean}: the decision summaries state a pediatric patient population, but the \textbf{performance data behind them is adult}. A population statement in an intended-use paragraph is a candidate for label review, not evidence of testing in children.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters operationally}: triage silently changes the order a pediatric radiologist sees cases in. A model tuned on adult prevalence will mis-prioritize a pediatric list, and nobody sees the ranking it produced.
\end{itemize}
\medskip
\textbf{Take-home}: ``FDA-cleared, pediatric population included'' is this market's most common and most misleading claim. Ask for pediatric subgroup performance; usually there is none.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[category=Commercial Software]{Product 4: deep-learning reconstruction --- the AI children actually meet}
\relax
{\scriptsize GE AIR Recon DL / TrueFidelity; Siemens Deep Resolve; Canon AiCE; Philips SmartSpeed / Precise Image\par}
\medskip
\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What it does}: reconstructs MRI and CT from noisier, faster or lower-dose raw data --- shorter MRI at comparable quality, CT at reduced dose. It is not interpretive: it makes the picture the radiologist then reads.
  \item \textbf{Why it is the biggest pediatric win on this list}: a shorter MRI is \textbf{fewer sedations}; a lower-dose CT is \textbf{less radiation to a growing child}. Neither requires anyone to trust a diagnostic model.
  \item \textbf{The regulatory shape is different}: these are sold across ages as part of the scanner, with vendor-published pediatric protocols rather than a pediatric indication. Philips holds 28 pediatric-label-positive entries, \textbf{one} of which is software; the rest are scanner labels listing pediatric imaging as an application.
  \item \textbf{The caveat}: dose and time reductions are not fixed properties of the product name --- they depend on task, patient size and configuration, and must be measured locally.
\end{itemize}
\medskip
\textbf{Take-home}: most of the pediatric benefit already installed here is non-interpretive AI nobody calls AI, and it is where a validation program should start.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[category=Reviews]{Kamran review: broader research, stronger validation}
\relax
{\scriptsize Kamran et al., \textit{Pediatric Radiology} 2026
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1007/s00247-025-06462-5}{doi 10.1007/s00247-025-06462-5})\par}
\medskip
\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A large but concentrated literature}: 789 pediatric-focused original articles (2005--August 2024); 91\% addressed image interpretation/diagnosis. Leading applications were bone age (88), pneumonia (65), and brain lesions/tumors (54).
  \item \textbf{Generalizability needs stronger evidence}: 65\% relied on local hospital datasets. The authors call for diverse, multicenter datasets and prospective validation across pediatric ages, sites, and disease-prevalence settings.
  \item \textbf{Broaden the research agenda}: communication, education, policy, and stakeholder perspectives were underrepresented. Include children, families, and clinical teams in development and evaluation.
\end{itemize}
\medskip
\textbf{Take-home}: prioritize representative data, validation, and neglected clinical needs. This review maps research activity; it does not compare model accuracy or establish clinical readiness.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[category=Reviews]{ACR white paper: the pediatric AI gap}
\relax
{\scriptsize Sammer et al., \textit{J Am Coll Radiol} 2023;20:730--737
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacr.2023.06.003}{doi 10.1016/j.jacr.2023.06.003})\par}
\medskip
\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scope}: ACR Pediatric AI Workgroup white paper framing limited access to pediatric AI as a health equity issue.
  \item \textbf{Availability}: at the time of the review, only 6 of more than 200 FDA-cleared tools in the ACR catalog indicated pediatric use (about 3\%).
  \item \textbf{Why adult AI may fail}: growth, disease patterns, and imaging protocols differ; published studies show uneven performance across pediatric subgroups.
  \item \textbf{Barriers}: scarce labeled pediatric data, consent and privacy concerns, and weak financial incentives for smaller pediatric markets.
  \item \textbf{Recommendations}: validate in children, disclose age-specific evidence, fund pediatric development, involve families, and protect pediatric workflows. The authors introduce \textit{Image IntelliGently} to advance this work.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[category=Reviews]{Multisociety statement: safe pediatric AI implementation}
\relax
{\scriptsize Shelmerdine et al., \textit{J Am Coll Radiol} 2026;23:89--101
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacr.2025.08.019}{doi 10.1016/j.jacr.2025.08.019})\par}
\medskip
{\small ACR, ESPR, SPR, SLARP, AOSPR, and SPIN propose four pillars for safe adoption:\par}
\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Regulation and purchasing}: pediatric labeling and proposed safety ratings; diverse datasets, subgroup performance, transparency, and cybersecurity.
  \item \textbf{Implementation and integration}: define a local clinical need, select tools validated in children, pilot before scaling, and involve pediatric specialists and families.
  \item \textbf{Interpretation and postmarket surveillance}: retain human oversight; audit performance and patient outcomes; manage discrepancies, report adverse events, and address automation bias.
  \item \textbf{Education}: foundational AI literacy for all staff, pediatric-specific training, continuing education, and patient/public engagement.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worth knowing as a radiologist}
\scriptsize
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Deep Learning: A Primer for Radiologists} (Radiographics paper, 2017): a helpful introduction to the principles and applications of deep learning in radiology; essential for understanding how AI tools work and their limitations
\item \textbf{RSNA Pediatric Bone Age Challenge (2017)} (benchmark / dataset): a landmark pediatric benchmark; useful for understanding automated bone-age accuracy and its limits
\item \textbf{Gleamer BoneView / AZmed Rayvolve} (clinical products): examples of fracture-detection assistance with pediatric indications; eligibility depends on age, anatomy, and product version
\item \textbf{FDA AI-enabled device list and linked labeling} (regulatory resource): a starting point for checking authorized use and pediatric age ranges; the list is not exhaustive
\item \textbf{CLAIM checklist (2024 update)} (reporting guideline): check whether data, reference standards, and testing are adequately described; reporting completeness alone does not establish clinical benefit
\item \textbf{TotalSegmentator} (research tool): automated CT / MR organ segmentation for volumes and body composition; check accuracy in children before trusting measurements
\item \textbf{nnU-Net} (research framework): a strong baseline for training segmentation models; ask whether a new method improves on an appropriate comparator
\item \textbf{MedSAM / segment-anything models} (research models): prompt-guided contours can assist annotation; radiologists still need to review and correct the output
\item \textbf{MONAI} (research framework): infrastructure for imaging-AI development; useful when collaborating with a research team, rather than a ready-to-use diagnostic tool
\item \textbf{NVIDIA NV-Reason-CT} (open research model, Sept 2026): a 3D CT vision-language model that drafts chest / abdominal CT findings with step-by-step reasoning and answers questions; open weights, research use only, not FDA-cleared, and no pediatric data or age range reported

\begin{frame}{Recommendations for pediatric radiologists}
\begin{itemize}
  \setlength{\itemsep}{12pt}
  \item AI development is constrained by scarce public datasets, fragmented institutional data, rare diseases, and heterogeneous reporting practices.
  \item Choose one task worth improving in your practice: bone-age measurements,
        fracture detection, or reducing CT dose / MRI scan time.
  \item Before adopting a tool, ask which children it was tested on. Check the
        intended age range and test it on your own cases, including younger children
        and unusual anatomy.
  \item In a local pilot, measure what changes with AI: reading time, missed findings,
        false alarms, dose, or scan time. Review errors and keep checking after rollout.
  \item Help build the evidence we need: choose a pediatric problem you see often,
        label cases carefully, and work with other hospitals to test whether a model
        works beyond the site that built it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
